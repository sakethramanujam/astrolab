Missed Known Planets in a TESS Sector 70 BLS Search: A Pre-v1.1 Failure Taxonomy

Saketh Ramanujam

Download draft.tex Download refs.bib

Abstract

We present a pre-v1.1 methods audit of why a single-sector Box-fitting Least Squares (BLS) search of TESS Sector 70 fails to recover already known planets. This Research Note is not a detection claim. Of 222 TESS Object of Interest (TOI) host stars, the single retained BLS peak period-matched a known TOI on 136 stars, and 106 of those also passed the pipeline vetting screens. The 86 period-match failures are classified at the in-pipeline screening floor of 7.0 and at the provisional Sector 70 threshold S* = 11.07 (95% confidence interval 9.32–13.32). Most misses have fewer than two transits in the sector or fall below threshold. A minority are caused by keeping one peak per star, a 0.75 day detrending window, or quality masking. We compare these results with recent TESS search and vetting studies and outline registered remedies for pipeline version 1.1.

1. Introduction

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS; Ricker et al. 2015) has assembled a large catalog of TESS Objects of Interest (TOIs; Guerrero et al. 2021). Independent Box-fitting Least Squares (BLS; Kovács et al. 2002) searches remain useful both for new candidates and for measuring completeness against that catalog. Completeness is often quoted as a single recovery fraction. Here we instead diagnose why known planets are missed.

The audit uses the public exosearch pipeline in the astrolab repository (github.com/sakethramanujam/astrolab, branch exoplanet-search, directory exoplanet_search/), frozen at git commit 68452b7 with configuration hash 80ffc7f29943. All counts below come from the Sector 70 clean audit of that tree. They predate pipeline version 1.1 and are not a planet detection or a Community TOI submission.

2. Pipeline and Audit

We search Science Processing Operations Center 2-minute PDCSAP light curves after a quality mask and gap-edge trim. Trends are removed with a wotan biweight filter and a 0.75 day window (Hippke et al. 2019). BLS is run from 0.5 days to half the sector baseline, and version 1.0 keeps one peak per star. The search statistic is

Sexact = depth × √(ntr) / CDPPD,

where ntr is the number of transits and CDPPD is the combined differential photometric precision evaluated at the trial duration D. The detection threshold S* is obtained from a generalized Pareto distribution fit to the tail of null (flux-flipped and scrambled) light curves. The provisional, pre-v1.1 Sector 70 value is S* = 11.07 (95% confidence interval 9.32–13.32), fit on 7,148 null stars; the frozen threshold will come from the v1.1 run. The in-pipeline screening floor is 7.0.

A host is counted as period-matched if the retained peak lies within 1% in period of a catalogued TOI. The 86 hosts that fail this test are assigned a single mutually exclusive cause, in fixed order (exosearch/missed.py): fewer than two transits of the TOI ephemeris with at least half of their cadences present in the raw sector data; a biweight window shorter than three times the TOI duration; at least two raw transits but fewer than two surviving the quality mask and gap-edge trim; S measured at the TOI ephemeris below the threshold under test; or S at that ephemeris at or above threshold, but a stronger peak retained because only one peak is kept per star.

3. Failure Taxonomy

Sector 70 contains 222 TOI host stars. The retained peak period-matched a known TOI on 136 of them; 106 of those also passed the pipeline vetting screens. Table 1 classifies the 86 period-match failures at both thresholds. Both columns sum to 86. The few-transit (25), masked (6), and detrending-window (2) counts do not depend on the threshold. Raising the cut from 7.0 to S* = 11.07 moves 7 of the 8 “outcompeted” hosts into the below-threshold bin (45 to 52, and 8 to 1), as expected when S at the TOI ephemeris lies between the two values.

We do not quote a recovery fraction at S* = 11.07. The 136 period matches describe the single retained peak and are not, in the frozen numbers used here, further split by whether that peak also exceeds S*.

4. Comparison with Recent Work

Lafarga et al. (2026) recover 2633 of 3098 TOIs (84.99%) with BLS on TESS full-frame-image light curves from Sectors 1–55, keeping the five highest BLS peaks and searching periods of 0.5–16 days after a 4 day Savitzky–Golay detrend. That ~85% recovery is therefore not a like-for-like comparison with a single-sector search that retains one peak per star.

Taaki et al. (2025) report a 73% recall rate, matching a known TOI to any above-threshold statistic, and a 63% detection rate, matching only the top statistic, for known TOIs in three years of TESS continuous viewing zone 2-minute data. Their detection rate is the closer analogue to our one-peak-per-star period match, but they search year-long light curves over periods of 1–100 days rather than a single TESS sector.

Kunimoto et al. (2025) and Valizadegan et al. (2025) address automated vetting and classification of already-detected transit-like signals, not BLS search completeness. The 91% completeness and 97% reliability of LEO-Vetter on simulated data, and the ExoMiner++ classification of 1797 of 2506 ExoFOP planet-candidate TOIs as planet candidates, should not be read as search-recovery fractions.

5. Outlook

The taxonomy points to specific, already registered changes for version 1.1, which are future work and are not reported here. An iterative mask-and-research step, run for 3–5 rounds with S* taken from the maximum over rounds, is intended to recover planets that lose the single-peak contest. The detrending window will be required to be at least three times the maximum trial duration. The noise-only threshold will be recalibrated with a segment-rotation scramble null, in which each contiguous segment between data gaps is cyclically rotated. A known-planet recovery test will be registered before the v1.1 search is run. Until that freeze, no signal from this pipeline is to be called a detection.
